Last.fm Lens

A magnifier for tidying up scrobbles: duplicate artists, combined artist names, inconsistent track titles (including likely typos), artist names repeated inside titles, missing or clashing albums, duplicate scrobbles and long-tail oddities. It edits nothing: it tells you what to look at, with the evidence in plain sight.

Drop your scrobble CSV here

The one from lastfmstats.com (lastfmstats-yourname.csv) works, as does any CSV with artist, album, track and date columns.

Everything is processed in your browser. The file is never uploaded anywhere.

How it works, and where its limits lie

Every finding is a scored suspicion from 0 to 100, with the clues that fired and the concrete evidence. The engine proposes; you decide. Nothing in this report asserts that something is definitely wrong.

It is all code

There is no language model and no external service in the analysis: only deterministic functions (normalised keys, sets of tracks, counts and a bounded edit distance) in src/engine.js. The same CSV always produces exactly the same report, and every rule can be read and changed.

Evidence rule

  • Sharing a track is the strong proof. An artist rename is only proposed as work to do when the two names share at least one track, or when they are identical bar capitalisation, accents or punctuation. Looking similar is not proof on its own, so a pair of names that are alike character by character but share no track is only ever an advisory question, never a rename to do. The old “they start with the same letters” rule was deleted: it produced pairs like Clara Bell / Claudia Belmonte.
  • Confidence bands: high from 60 points, medium from 40, low below that (RULES.highThreshold / RULES.mediumThreshold in src/engine.js).
  • Order: inside a section the findings are grouped by sub-case, and the groups are ordered by their strongest finding, so the highest-scoring ones come first. Within a group the order is confidence, then impact, then points. The other two sort modes replace the sections with a single flat list ordered by impact or by confidence.
  • Nothing is dropped in silence. Checks that are made and not listed are counted in the summary at the top: artist-name pairs with no track in common, album clashes below the minimum impact, album-name variants past the cap, and findings under the minimum confidence. The last group can be brought back with minimumConfidence: 'low'.
  • Each section can be switched off. The section chips are switches, not a single choice: click one to take that section out of the list (Duplicate scrobbles, say), click it again to bring it back, and “Everything” switches them all back on. A section that is off is dimmed and struck through. It matters most in the two flat sort modes, where the sections are not drawn at all and the dimmed chip is the only sign that one is missing. The choice is remembered per report, next to your Accept / Discard marks. In the two flat sort modes, where the sections are not drawn, the heading names the sections that are off and carries a “Show them” button.
  • Advisory findings are marked. Some cards say “this may well be correct, ignore it” (a single and an album are different releases, or a live recording and the studio one, for instance). They are labelled advisory and “Actionable only” hides them, so the count of things to do stays honest.

What each section looks at

  • Duplicate artists: two names pointing at the same artist. The strong proof is sharing tracks: if every track under “Ana” also sits under “Ana Beltrán”, it is almost certainly the same entry. It also counts whether their scrobbles mix within the same sitting (less than ten minutes apart). Sub-cases: identical names bar formatting, one name contained in the other, several forms of the same artist, very similar names that share tracks and very similar names with no shared track. The last two also compare the two names character by character, because a misspelling with more than a couple of scrobbles used to be invisible: “Jana Burčeska” against “Jana Burcheska” is proposed when they share a track, and merely asked about when they do not. Two names that differ only by an underscore, a double space or a comma (“Senza_Cri” against “Senza Cri”) are identical as far as the engine is concerned, so they need no threshold at all. It also catches an abbreviation, where one name is the beginning of the other and every other word is the same (“Cris Lora” against “Cristina Lora”), while keeping out pairs like “Clara Bell” / “Claudia Belmonte”, where two words differ at once.
  • Combined artists: names such as “Ana, Bruno Salas y Cata”. These are flagged only when the first artist of the name exists separately in your library, and the proposal is exactly that first one (“Ana”). If the first one does not exist, the first that does is used, with a warning that it may not be the main artist. The same name written without the separator (“Ana Bruno”) is swept into the same finding, because it is the same artist spelled differently.
  • Duplicates never merge into a combined name. If the most frequent form of a pair is itself a combined name, nothing is merged into it: everything is renamed straight to the artist it stands for. That is why “Ana Bruno” points at “Ana” and never at “Ana, Bruno”, and why a name already covered by a combined-name finding produces only one card rather than two saying the same thing.
  • Inconsistent track titles: the same track written in several ways under the same artist. Sub-cases: formatting only, capitalisation only, qualifier written differently, featuring credit in one form only, probably a typo in the title, titles carrying a platform badge, probably a different version (live, remix…), with and without a qualifier, different qualifiers. A platform badge is the junk a video page adds to an upload: “Song (Official Video)”, “Song (Official Audio)”, “Song (Lyric Video)”, “Song (Video Oficial)”, “Song (Audio)”. It is worth a card of its own because it points at where the scrobble came from — YouTube or Vevo rather than a music player — and because it is invisible when every scrobble of a track carries it. The proposal only ever removes the badge, never adds a credit, and the badge check is strict: the whole qualifier has to be the badge, so real credits such as “from the Prime Video Original Movie” or “Official Song UEFA Euro 2016” stay where they are. Capitalisation alone is not damage: a name written twice with different capitals gets its own sub-case, is advisory and never counts as work, because nothing is broken and nothing is lost. An accent that went wrong, a broken quote or a stray space does count: those keep the high score. A qualifier such as live, remix, acoustic or version marks a different recording, so those cases get their own group, are marked advisory (they are decisions to make, not merges to do) and never appear among the plain title variants. Their proposal prefers the plain form as the target, because the qualified one explicitly claims something the others do not. The typo check is deliberately narrow: same artist, both titles starting with the same letter, and only when the rarer spelling is genuinely rare (at most three scrobbles). So “Patata” against “Patataa” or “Papata” is proposed, while “Lugar I” against “Lugar II” is not: numbering is not a typo. It only sees spelling, so the card warns you when a similar name could be a different song.
  • Artist repeated in title: the Artist field already says “Ana” and the title repeats it (“Ana - Something”). Typical of a badly tagged file or an import. The proposal removes the repeated artist from the title; it only fires when the title really starts with the artist’s name followed by a dash or a colon, so it stays quiet about titles that merely mention it. When the same title also ends with a platform badge (“Ana - Something (Official Video)”), one single card proposes the whole fix, badge included, instead of two cards proposing half a fix each.
  • Albums: sub-cases no album (another scrobble has one), album name written in several ways, capitalisation only and several albums for the same title.
    As with titles, two spellings that differ only in capitalisation are the same album either way, so they are advisory and never counted as work. “Several albums for the same title” may be perfectly correct (the single and the album are different things), so it is advisory too, and never counted as work to do. An album-name variant is only ever proposed inside one artist, because an album is an (artist, album) pair: “Amnesia” by one artist and “AMNESIA” by another are two different albums, not two spellings of one, and merging them would rewrite somebody else's album. When the two spellings belong to two spellings of the same artist, that is the duplicate-artist section's business: rename the artist first.
  • Duplicate scrobbles: the same track twice in a row less than a minute apart. This is the one section where the finding is a near-certainty rather than a suspicion.
  • Long tail and oddities: sub-cases probably a typo in the artist name, formatting details inside tags, scrobbles with no album and artists with two scrobbles or fewer; the last three are informational lists, not mistakes.

What it does NOT do

  • It does not edit your CSV and it does not touch your Last.fm account.
  • It does not decide for you: where two readings are possible, the finding says so explicitly.
  • It does not know any artist’s real discography. “They share 15 tracks” is very good evidence, but it is still an inference from your own data.

The “good” value when there are two candidates

  • By default the proposal is the name that combines being the most recent, the most frequent and the most complete, and the card shows all three so you can disagree with it. If one of the three points the other way, a warning appears.
  • The rule can be changed in src/engine.js (RULES.canonical: recent, frequent or score).

Making the changes in Last.fm

  • Edits to artist, track and album are retroactive: when you edit one scrobble, Last.fm offers to apply it to every scrobble of that combination (and renaming an artist affects the whole entry). It is a bulk change: look twice.
  • Deleting a duplicate scrobble is one at a time, from the scrobble’s (⋯) menu.
  • Accept marks a finding as to do: it gets a green edge and appears in the to-do list, which you download with its own button. Discard fades the card, stops listing it and drops its row from actions.csv. Both can be undone with “Back to pending”. Neither of them touches your Last.fm account, and the Markdown and JSON downloads do not carry them: those are the report, not your working list.

Privacy

  • The analysis runs entirely in your browser. No server, no upload, no network requests.
